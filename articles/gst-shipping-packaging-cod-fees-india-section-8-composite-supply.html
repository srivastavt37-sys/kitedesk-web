GST on Shipping, Packaging & COD Handling Fees in India: Section 8 Composite Supply Rules for D2C Bills

November 24, 2026 • 9 min read • 2085 words • Plain-English Playbook
WATCH OUR ORIGINAL AI-NARRATED WALKTHROUGH

A Surat-based women's ethnic-wear brand shipping 300 direct orders a month noticed a quiet leak in its monthly GSTR-3B filing. Every cotton kurta on the store sold at ₹899 (5% GST slab under HSN 6204), and the checkout added a flat ₹75 shipping fee plus a ₹25 Cash-on-Delivery handling fee on COD parcels. The founder's accounting freelancer had configured the invoicing sheet to tax goods at 5% and tax the ₹100 combined shipping and COD fee as a standalone courier service under SAC 9965 at 18%.

That single configuration error over-charged GST by ₹13.00 on every COD order—₹3,900 every month, or ₹46,800 a year—while forcing the customer to pay a higher landed price and creating unnecessary two-rate rows in GSTR-1. Under Section 8(a) of the Central Goods and Services Tax (CGST) Act, 2017, shipping, protective packaging, and COD handling billed by the seller as part of delivering a product are not standalone courier services. They form a composite supply whose tax rate follows the principal goods being sold.

The ₹899 Apparel + ₹75 Shipping Math: 5% vs 18% GST

Most Indian D2C founders confuse two different transactions: what Shiprocket or Delhivery charges you on their B2B freight invoice (which carries 18% GST under SAC 9965/9968, claimable as Input Tax Credit if you are registered under regular GST), and what you charge your retail buyer on the customer tax invoice.

When you bill a customer ₹899 for a cotton kurta plus ₹75 for standard shipping, the customer is not buying a courier service from you independently of the garment. Delivery is naturally bundled with the sale of the kurta. Let us compare the exact numbers on a single order and across 300 monthly orders.

On a ₹75 shipping charge alone, taxing freight at 18% instead of 5% adds ₹9.75 of excess GST per parcel. Across 300 orders a month, that equals ₹2,925 in avoidable tax outflow. Once you include a ₹25 packaging or COD handling fee (bringing ancillary charges to ₹100 per parcel), the 13-percentage-point gap (18% minus 5%) equals ₹13.00 per order, or ₹3,900 per month.

If your store absorbs GST inside an all-inclusive checkout price (say ₹999 free-shipping-style or ₹1,020 flat), that ₹13.00 per order comes straight out of your net contribution margin. Section 8(a) Composite Supply vs Section 8(b) Mixed Supply

Indian GST law draws a sharp line between a composite supply and a mixed supply. Getting this distinction right protects you during GST departmental scrutiny and keeps your BillFold invoices audit-ready.

When Shipping & Packaging Qualify as Composite Supply

Under Section 2(30) and Section 8(a) of the CGST Act, 2017, a supply is a composite supply when three conditions hold:

  • Two or more taxable supplies of goods or services are naturally bundled and supplied in conjunction with each other in the ordinary course of business.
  • One of those supplies is a principal supply (the main item the buyer actually came to purchase, such as apparel, footwear, books, or packaged food).
  • The ancillary items—corrugated box, bubble wrap, courier delivery, or COD collection handling—exist only to complete delivery of that principal item.

When all three apply, Section 8(a) mandates that the entire bundle is taxed at the GST rate of the principal supply. If you sell printed books (0% GST), delivery charged on that same invoice is 0%. If you sell apparel priced up to ₹1,000 (5% GST), shipping and packing on that bill are taxed at 5%. If you sell Bluetooth earbuds or skincare serums (18% GST), shipping is taxed at 18%.

When a Bundle Triggers Mixed Supply (The Highest-Rate Trap)

Section 8(b) covers mixed supply: two or more individual items sold together for a single price that are not naturally bundled (for example, a festive gift box containing ₹400 of dry fruits at 5%/12%, a ₹300 scented candle at 18%, and a ₹250 ceramic mug at 12%, all sold as one "Diwali Hamper" SKU for ₹999).

In a mixed supply, there is no single principal supply, so the law taxes the entire ₹999 hamper—plus any ₹80 shipping charge—at the highest applicable rate among the items (18% in that example). To avoid paying 18% on low-tax food or apparel items, itemize distinct products as separate line items on your invoice whenever they are not naturally dependent on each other.

What Section 15(2)(c) Says About Packaging & COD Handling Fees

Many sellers assume that if they label a fee "Reimbursement of Courier Charges," "Gift Wrap Fee," or "COD Cash Collection Fee," they can either skip GST on it or leave it off the main taxable base. Section 15(2)(c) of the CGST Act closes that loophole explicitly: the value of supply includes incidental expenses, including commission and packing, charged by the supplier to the recipient for anything done in respect of the supply at the time of or before delivery.

That means three everyday D2C line items must sit inside your taxable base before GST is calculated:

  1. Forward Shipping / Delivery Charges: Whether you charge ₹49, ₹75, or ₹120, it adds directly to the taxable value.
  2. Protective or Gift Packaging Fees: Rigid gift boxes, tamper-proof courier bags, or cold-chain gel packs billed separately to the buyer are incidental packing expenses under Section 15(2)(c).
  3. COD Handling / Convenience Fees: When a buyer chooses Cash on Delivery and pays an extra ₹30 or ₹50 fee, that fee is part of the consideration for supplying the order. It takes the principal supply's GST rate (or the highest rate in a multi-slab cart if not split pro-rata).

Why the "Pure Agent" Rule (Rule 33) Rarely Protects D2C Brands

Every few months, a seller asks whether they can exclude ₹75 shipping from GST altogether by claiming to act as a "pure agent" between the buyer and Blue Dart or Delhivery under Rule 33 of the CGST Rules, 2017. In practice, almost no D2C brand qualifies as a pure agent, for four strict legal reasons:

  • You contract on your own account: Your rate card is signed between your business and the courier aggregator, not between the buyer and the courier.
  • Flat-rate markup or subsidy: Rule 33 requires recovering the exact actual amount paid to the third party without any margin or rounding. Charging a flat ₹75 when the actual airway bill costs ₹64.20 or ₹88.50 immediately disqualifies pure-agent status.
  • Separate authorization: The buyer would need to explicitly authorize you to pay the courier on their behalf and hold an invoice issued in the buyer's name—impossible in standard D2C checkouts.
  • ITC conflict: You cannot claim 18% Input Tax Credit on the courier's B2B invoice while treating the same freight as a pure-agent pass-through.

Treating shipping as part of the taxable base under Section 8 composite supply is cleaner, faster, and fully compliant with regular B2C and B2B invoicing.

Handling Multi-Slab Carts Without GSTR-1 Mismatches

What happens when a single customer order contains two products in different GST slabs—for example, a ₹899 cotton shirt (5% GST) and a ₹1,499 leather belt (18% GST)—plus ₹80 shipping? If you simply apply 18% to the ₹80 shipping without a documented rule, or worse, apply 5% to the whole bill, your GSTR-1 HSN summary will fail validation or over-charge the buyer.

Accountants handle multi-slab carts in one of two compliant ways:

Approach How It Works on ₹899 (5%) + ₹1,499 (18%) + ₹80 Ship Best Used When
Option A: Pro-Rata Split Allocate ₹80 shipping by item value: ₹30.00 at 5% (₹1.50 GST) and ₹50.00 at 18% (₹9.00 GST). Total shipping GST = ₹10.50. Automated ERP / Shopify-to-accounting sync where line-item tax apportionment runs automatically.
Option B: Highest-Rate Conservative Rule Tax the ₹80 shipping at the highest product slab in the cart (18% = ₹14.40 GST), keeping goods at their own slabs (5% and 18%). Manual B2B/WhatsApp billing where simplicity and zero under-payment risk matter most.

Notice that for single-slab carts (which represent 75% to 85% of orders for focused D2C apparel, footwear, beauty, or home-goods stores), you never need pro-rata math at all. Every item and the shipping charge share one uniform GST rate.

Fixing GSTR-1 Table 12 & GSTR-3B Reconciliation

Why do CAs flag split-rate shipping invoices during quarterly audits? Because when an invoicing tool logs ₹899 under HSN 6204 at 5% and ₹75 under SAC 9965 at 18%:

  • GSTR-1 Table 12 (HSN-wise Summary): You end up reporting tiny ₹75 SAC 9965 entries across hundreds of B2C invoices. If the portal rounds tax decimals slightly differently per row than your GSTR-3B summary, you get a red variance alert.
  • B2B Buyer ITC Confusion: When a corporate gifting client buys 50 kurtas at 5% GST and sees a separate 18% freight line on the same tax invoice, their accounts payable team often holds payment asking why SAC 9965 was billed without a separate freight bill.
  • Credit Notes & Partial Returns: Issuing a credit note for a damaged item becomes messy when shipping was taxed at a different rate than the returned SKU.

When you fold shipping and packaging directly into the invoice's taxable base at the principal product's GST rate, your entire invoice carries one clean tax rate, one CGST/SGST (or IGST) split, and one unambiguous HSN total.

6-Point Pre-Dispatch GST Billing Checklist

  1. Confirm your principal supply HSN rate: Verify whether your core SKU sits in 5%, 12%, or 18% GST—especially for apparel around the ₹1,000 threshold.
  2. Include shipping, packing, and COD fees in the taxable base: Never mark seller-billed courier fees as "0% exempt reimbursement" on a taxable order.
  3. Match shipping GST to the principal product rate on single-slab orders: Stop defaulting to 18% SAC 9965 on 5% or 12% goods.
  4. Use intra-state vs inter-state split accurately: Match the first two digits of your GSTIN against the customer's state of delivery (CGST+SGST for same state, IGST for different state).
  5. Check your unit economics with shipping + RTO factored in: Run your landed price, courier rate, and return percentage through MarginMint before running free-shipping promos.
  6. Push prepaid UPI over COD: Include a scannable BillFold or TapPing UPI QR on order confirmations to cut COD handling fees and RTO leakage together.
