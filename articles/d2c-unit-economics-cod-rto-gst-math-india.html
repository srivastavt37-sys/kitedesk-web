The Real Unit Economics of Selling Physical Products in India: COGS, GST, Courier Weight Slabs, and COD RTO Math

1. Why Paper Profits Disappear Before Payout Day

If you manufacture or source a physical product in India—whether it is handcrafted leather jootis, cotton kurtas, specialty coffee, or desk accessories—the most dangerous number in your business is gross markup. A founder buys or crafts a unit for Rs 540, lists it on an Instagram store or Shopify page for Rs 1,499, and assumes there is nearly Rs 960 of room to pay for shipping and ads. Two months later, despite shipping 300 parcels a month, the bank account is flat.

The leak rarely comes from one big expense. It comes from five separate deductions that hit every single order before the money settles in your current account: inclusive Goods and Services Tax (GST), corrugated box and insert materials, forward courier weight slabs, payment gateway or Cash-on-Delivery (COD) collection fees, and Return-to-Origin (RTO) courier penalties on undelivered parcels.

When you model these numbers on the back of a notebook, you usually calculate them on a single successful prepaid order. Real Indian e-commerce does not run on 100% prepaid orders. Across tier-2 and tier-3 pin codes—and even in metro cities for new brands—60% to 75% of buyers still pick Cash on Delivery. And a predictable percentage of those COD parcels will come back to your warehouse.

  • Selling Price (MRP Paid by Customer): Rs 1,499 (inclusive of 12% GST)
  • Net Revenue Before Tax: Rs 1,338.39 (Rs 160.61 goes straight to GST output liability)
  • Product Making Cost (COGS): Rs 540.00 per pair
  • Rigid Box, Tissue, Thank-You Card, and Tape: Rs 35.00 per parcel
  • Forward Courier Charge (500g volumetric slab, Zone C/D average): Rs 85.00
  • Payment Gateway / COD Collection Fee (2.5%): Rs 37.48

2. Inclusive GST Math: Why 12% GST Is Not 12% of Your Cost

A surprisingly common mistake among first-time sellers is forgetting that retail prices in India are almost always tax-inclusive. When a customer pays Rs 1,499 on UPI or COD, they are not paying Rs 1,499 plus tax; the tax is already baked inside the Rs 1,499.

To find the actual revenue you keep, you divide the selling price by 1 plus the tax rate: Rs 1,499 / 1.12 = Rs 1,338.39. That means Rs 160.61 is GST collected from the buyer. Even if you claim Input Tax Credit (ITC) on your raw leather, fabric, boxes, and courier bills (which carry 18% GST), your net cash outflow to tax and compliance still eats a real slice of the top line.

If your product falls in the 18% slab—such as bags, electronics accessories, or certain home goods—the extraction is even steeper: Rs 1,499 / 1.18 = Rs 1,270.34, leaving Rs 228.66 as tax component. Before you spend a single rupee on shipping or marketing, your real top line is already Rs 1,270 to Rs 1,338, not Rs 1,499.

  • 5% GST Slab Formula: Selling Price - (Selling Price / 1.05)
  • 12% GST Slab Formula: Selling Price - (Selling Price / 1.12)
  • 18% GST Slab Formula: Selling Price - (Selling Price / 1.18)
  • Always price your catalog backwards from net post-tax realization, never from raw MRP.

3. The Hidden Multiplier of Cash-on-Delivery (COD) RTO

Now let us look at what happens when 15% to 25% of your orders fail delivery. Suppose you ship 100 orders at Rs 1,499. Eighty-five customers accept the parcel and pay. Fifteen customers reject the parcel at the doorstep, do not pick up the delivery executive's call, or entered an incomplete address without a landmark.

On those 15 returned orders, you do not lose the product itself (assuming it returns undamaged), so your Rs 540 COGS is recovered when the box reaches your shelf. And you do not pay GST or payment gateway fees on a cancelled sale. However, the courier aggregator—whether you use Shiprocket, Delhivery, BlueDart, or Xpressbees—charges you the Rs 85 forward shipping fee plus a return shipping fee (typically 70% to 100% of forward shipping, around Rs 68 to Rs 85). On top of that, the outer shipping carton, tape, and shipping label (worth Rs 35) are usually crushed or taped over and have to be replaced.

That means every single RTO parcel costs you roughly Rs 85 (forward) + Rs 68 (reverse) + Rs 35 (wasted packaging) = Rs 188 in dead cash. Across 15 failed orders out of 100, you lose Rs 2,820. Because only 85 delivered orders generate profit to pay for that loss, each delivered order has to absorb Rs 2,820 / 85 = Rs 33.18 of RTO drag.

  • At 10% RTO rate: ~Rs 20.89 dead courier/packaging drag per delivered order
  • At 15% RTO rate: ~Rs 33.18 dead courier/packaging drag per delivered order
  • At 25% RTO rate: ~Rs 62.67 dead courier/packaging drag per delivered order
  • At 35% RTO rate: ~Rs 101.23 dead courier/packaging drag per delivered order

4. Volumetric Weight Traps in Courier Billing

Footwear, gift boxes, and home decor sellers get hit by a second courier surprise: volumetric weight. Courier companies bill you on whichever is higher—actual dead weight on the scale or volumetric weight calculated as Length (cm) x Breadth (cm) x Height (cm) / 5000.

A pair of embroidered jootis might weigh only 420 grams on a kitchen scale, which fits inside the base 500-gram courier slab (Rs 65 to Rs 85 depending on zone). But if you pack that pair inside a tall, oversized shoe box measuring 32 cm x 22 cm x 12 cm, the volumetric calculation is (32 x 22 x 12) / 5000 = 1.689 kg. The courier system automatically bumps your shipment into the 2.0 kg slab, doubling your shipping cost from Rs 85 to Rs 165+.

Trimming just 2 centimeters off your box height—switching to a snug 29 cm x 18 cm x 9.5 cm die-cut mailer box—brings volumetric weight down to 0.99 kg (or under 500g for compact pouches), saving Rs 40 to Rs 80 on every single dispatch without changing the product inside.

5. Break-Even ROAS: Knowing Your Maximum Ad Spend Ceiling

Once we subtract all real costs from our Rs 1,499 order at a 15% RTO rate, what is actually left? Start with Rs 1,499. Subtract Rs 160.61 (12% GST), Rs 540.00 (COGS), Rs 85.00 (forward shipping), Rs 35.00 (packaging), Rs 37.48 (2.5% fee), and Rs 33.18 (15% RTO drag). Your true net profit per delivered order is Rs 607.73—a 40.5% net take-home margin before marketing.

That Rs 607.73 is the absolute maximum you can spend to acquire one delivered customer before you start losing money. In advertising terms, your Break-Even Return on Ad Spend (ROAS) is Selling Price / Net Profit = Rs 1,499 / Rs 607.73 = 2.47x. If your Meta or Google ad agency reports a 2.1x ROAS and congratulates you on 'doubling your ad money,' you are actually losing Rs 90 on every order shipped.

If you want to run a business with zero paid ad spend, you keep that entire Rs 607.73 per order by driving customers through organic Google search, Google Business Profile, and direct WhatsApp repeat orders using a printed QR code inside every box.

6. Worked Case Study: 500 Monthly Orders Across Prepaid vs COD Mix

To see how small unit-economics adjustments compound across a full month, compare two Indian D2C brands selling the exact same product at Rs 1,499 with a Rs 540 manufacturing cost. Brand A runs default settings: oversized boxes (Rs 125 shipping), no COD confirmation workflow (28% RTO rate), and no repeat-order QR insert inside the box. Brand B uses compact die-cut boxes (Rs 80 shipping), sends a WhatsApp confirmation message with a Rs 50 UPI prepayment incentive via BillFold and TapPing (bringing RTO down to 11%), and places a reorder QR sticker inside every box lid.

At 500 dispatched orders a month, Brand A sees 140 parcels return undelivered. Those 140 returns burn Rs 32,900 in wasted two-way freight and crushed boxes. Brand B sees only 55 returns, cutting dead freight loss down to Rs 10,725—an immediate saving of Rs 22,175 every single month without raising prices by a single rupee.

Even better, because Brand B saves Rs 45 per parcel on volumetric shipping across the 445 delivered orders (another Rs 20,025 saved), Brand B nets over Rs 42,200 more monthly take-home profit on the exact same 500-order volume. That is the difference between a stressed founder who thinks e-commerce is broken and a calm operator who knows their numbers.
